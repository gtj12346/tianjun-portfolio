03 — AI + GIS / FIELD NOTES

A pin on a map, and the journey behind it

My product internship at Beijing De’an IoT — conversion journeys, user research and AI + GIS.

Beijing De’an IoT Technology · Product Intern (AI / GIS)
June–August 2025 · Beijing
My role: defining problems, designing solutions and validating releases for GIS / smart-city products, alongside use-case and competitor research for AI + GIS
Keywords: conversion funnels · competitor research · prioritisation · event tracking and metrics · usability testing · SuperMap · GeoAI
Results: 15% relative increase in click-through rate (CTR) and 12% in conversion rate (CVR), comparing two-week periods before and after the releases

01The short version

Some pins on the company's GIS platform were different: they were ad placements, not ordinary locations. A user would see a pin, click it, open a landing page and submit a form to request a demo or an enquiry. Only a successful form submission counted as a conversion. Calls and detail views were tracked separately.

My main job that summer was to follow that journey and ask: Why weren't people clicking? Why weren't the people who clicked staying? Then turn the answers into proposals, help get those proposals shipped, and check the data to see whether our thinking held up.

02Why this needed work

When I joined, the problems were already on the table. Nobody had fully broken them down yet:

  • Low CTR: the ad pins received plenty of impressions, but few clicks.
  • High bounce rate: many people who reached the landing page left without doing anything.

On their own, these numbers just looked disappointing. Together, they pointed to a much bigger problem: lose people at the start, lose more further along, and very few make it to the end. That final number was what advertisers cared about.

My mentor's first task for me was simple: Don't jump to solutions. First, find out where people are leaving.

03My role

I was a product intern. I didn't make the final decisions, but I needed to prepare what people needed to make them. That meant four things:

  1. Product discovery and problem definition: map user journeys and study competitors, turning outcomes like low CTR and high bounce rates into specific questions about where and why people dropped off.
  2. Solution design and delivery: propose changes for those points of friction, prioritise by business impact and development effort, and work with engineering and business colleagues through review, development, testing and release.
  3. Validation and iteration: monitor core metrics including CTR, CVR and bounce rate, then use behaviour data and usability testing to evaluate the changes and find what still needed work.
  4. AI + GIS product planning: research use cases and competitors in GeoAI, spatial data analysis, map visualisation and decision support, using SuperMap's capabilities to inform future product plans.

The first three formed one complete delivery cycle. The fourth looked further ahead. I'll cover them separately.

04First, draw the funnel

One journey, five stages

I started by breaking the journey from seeing an ad to completing a conversion into five steps, each tied to a trackable event:

Valid impression → Click → Landing page loaded → Engaged view → Conversion
        └── CTR ────┘              │                   │            │
                    └─ Arrival rate ┘                   │            │
                                   └─ 1 − Bounce rate ──┘            │
                    └────────────────── CVR ─────────────────────────┘
  • CTR = clicks / valid impressions
  • Arrival rate = successful landing-page loads / clicks
  • Bounce rate = visits that ended without any interaction / landing-page arrivals
  • CVR = conversions / clicks

We deliberately used clicks, rather than landing-page visits, as the denominator for CVR. Using visits would quietly exclude people who gave up before a slow page loaded, making CVR look better than it was. Using clicks kept those losses in the picture.

Drawing the funnel made one thing clear: clicking and arriving aren't the same thing. Someone could click an ad and close the page before it loaded. That loss didn't show up as a CTR problem or a bounce-rate problem, so it was easy for both sides to overlook. It became one of our clearest reasons to improve load speed.

Competitors: what makes people take the next step?

I studied five products: Google Maps, Amap (including its developer platform), Baidu Maps, Tencent Maps and Dianping. The first four showed how ads appeared on maps. Dianping wasn't a map product, but its list / map → business card → details → booking journey offered a useful view of what happens after the first click. My method was straightforward: walk through each journey myself, take screenshots, count the steps and note what appeared on each screen.

A few observations stayed with me:

  • Ad pins need to look different. Google Maps used square promoted pins alongside ordinary round markers, making them easy to distinguish. Amap gave participating merchants more prominent markers, while non-participating ones appeared in grey. Our ad pins looked much like regular points of interest (POIs). At a glance, users couldn't tell which ones were worth clicking.
  • A click doesn't have to take you away immediately. Most map products opened an information card first. Users could then decide whether to continue. That extra layer actually made clicking feel less risky: if it wasn't useful, they could simply close it.
  • Keep the action close to the user. Google Maps tracked detail views, directions and tap-to-call actions separately for map ads. Amap also offered direct calls, lead-capture pages and forms. Action buttons were on the card, not buried at the bottom of a landing page.
  • Speed is part of conversion. In a 2017 analysis of 900,000 mobile ad landing pages, Google found that increasing load time from one to three seconds raised the probability of a bounce by 32%. Increasing page elements from 400 to 6,000 reduced conversion probability by 95%. I came back to those findings several times in reviews.

The problem list

After tracing our own journey and comparing it with competitors, I had a clearer list of issues. I ranked them by how many people they affected and how serious the impact was:

Stage Problem What it looked like
Impression → Click Ad pins were hard to distinguish; feedback was weak Pins looked like ordinary POIs. There was no immediate response to a click, so users weren't sure it had registered.
Click → Arrival Slow landing-page loads Large hero images, carousels and all page modules loaded at once, leaving a long blank screen on weak connections.
Arrival → Engaged view Too much information Company introductions, credentials and product lists filled the first screen. Users couldn't see what to do next.
Engaged view → Conversion Calls to action appeared too late Enquiry and lead-capture buttons were at the bottom, beyond where most people scrolled.

Two examples made the problems especially clear:

  • Our ad pins used the same icon as ordinary POIs, with just a tiny “Promoted” badge in the top-right corner. At street-level zoom, the badge was almost invisible.
  • A banner carousel took up most of the first mobile screen, followed by the company introduction and certificates. The “Request a demo” button was roughly three screens down.

05The plan: three changes, in the right order

1. Clearer ad interactions

The goal was to make ads easy to recognise and reassuring to click.

  • Distinctive pin styling: give ad pins their own shape and colour, without overwhelming the map. The map was a tool first; attention-grabbing ads could damage the whole experience.
  • Immediate click feedback: add a pressed state and highlight as soon as someone clicked, so they knew the action had registered.
  • A card before a redirect: open an information card on the map, with a title, one-line benefit, distance and action buttons. People who wanted more detail could continue to the landing page.

2. A simpler landing page

The goal was fast to load and easy to understand.

I set one rule for the first screen: it should answer just three questions—what is this, why should I care, and what can I do next? Everything else could be collapsed or moved further down.

  • Compress the hero image, lazy-load images below the fold and replace the carousel with one image.
  • Move the company introduction and certificates out of the first screen.
  • Show only the product relevant to that ad, rather than the full product list.

This change drew the most debate. Every module had someone who thought it mattered, so removing anything required an explanation. The load-time tracking, the number of elements on the first screen and Google's research helped make the case.

3. Bring the next action forward

The goal was to let people act as soon as they were ready.

  • Put two buttons on the information card: “View details” for the landing page and “Request a demo” to open the form directly, letting ready users skip the landing page.
  • Put the main “Request a demo” button on the first screen, with a sticky “Call us” button at the bottom as users scrolled.
  • Cut the lead form down to essential fields.

Setting priorities

We wanted all three changes, but engineering time was limited. I plotted business impact against development effort and reviewed the options with engineering and business colleagues:

Proposal Expected impact Development effort Priority
Simpler landing page and faster loading High: affects arrival and bounce rates Medium P0
Pin styling and click feedback Medium: directly affects CTR Low P0
Information card and earlier calls to action High: affects CVR High: new component and tracking P1

The logic was simple: do the useful, low-effort work first; break useful but expensive work into smaller pieces; skip work that adds little value. The information card required changes to the map component and new tracking events. We split it into stages, starting with a minimal title-and-buttons version and adding content after validation.

We shipped in two releases:

  • Early July: pin styling and click feedback, plus a simpler, faster landing page. Neither P0 change needed a new component, so both fitted into one iteration.
  • Late July: a minimal information card with a title, one-line benefit and two buttons, earlier calls to action, and a shorter form.
  • August: monitor the data and make smaller fixes based on the second round of usability testing.

From review to release

Once the plan was agreed, it followed a familiar but demanding path: requirements → review → design and development → testing → release.

As an intern, I focused on three things:

  • Make requirements testable: define what “done” meant for each change—for example, a highlight appearing within X milliseconds of a click, or only three types of content appearing on the first screen.
  • Include tracking in the requirements: agree event names, triggers and fields with engineering during review, rather than adding them after launch. Without tracking, we'd be flying blind on release day.
  • Walk through the journey myself: test on different phones and connections, catching obvious problems before passing the work to the testing team.

06After launch: watch the numbers and the people

Monitoring

Before launch, I put the main metrics into a daily dashboard:

  • Primary metrics: CTR, CVR and bounce rate
  • Process metrics: landing-page arrival rate, first-screen load time and first-screen action-button click rate
  • Guardrail metrics: ordinary POI click rate and time spent on the map, to check that more visible ads weren't hurting the map experience

My mentor suggested the guardrails. As they put it, if ad metrics improve but users feel the map has become cluttered, that's borrowing against the experience, not improving it.

Usability testing

Data could show where something was wrong, but not always why. We ran two rounds of usability testing: one in the test environment before the first release, and one after the second release. Each involved five people—a mix of colleagues outside the project and client-side users recruited through the business team. We gave them a task, such as finding a service provider on the map and booking a demo, then watched where they got stuck.

Five wasn't an arbitrary choice. Nielsen's well-known finding suggested that five users in a round of qualitative testing could uncover roughly 85% of usability problems. Rather than test 15 people at once, the idea was to run three rounds of five, making improvements between rounds.

Some of the issues were small, but very real:

  • Round one: two of the five participants couldn't identify the ad pins. Another scrolled through several screens of the old landing page and said, “I can't find how to contact them.” Both observations supported the first release's changes.
  • Round two: someone mistook the information card for an ordinary place listing and closed it without noticing “Request a demo”. We changed it from a text link to a solid primary button. On a small phone, the sticky call button covered the form's submit button. The industry dropdown was also too long; after watching someone struggle to find their industry, we shortened it to common options plus “Other”.

These findings went into the August iteration.

Results

By the end, CTR was up 15% and CVR was up 12%, both relative increases.

Here's how we measured them:

  • Comparison: before and after release. The platform had no A/B testing infrastructure, and building traffic splitting just for these changes would have cost more than the changes themselves.
  • Observation window: the two weeks before the first release versus the two weeks after the second release was fully rolled out.
  • Definitions: CTR = clicks / valid impressions; CVR = successful form submissions / clicks. Both reported changes were relative.

Definitions matter here. A “15% increase” could mean a relative increase, such as CTR going from 2.0% to 2.3%, or an absolute increase of 15 percentage points, from 2% to 17%. Those are very different claims. Every number in my report included its denominator, comparison method and observation window.

A before-and-after comparison has limits, too. Ad creative and budgets may change between periods, so we couldn't attribute the entire improvement to our work. We tried to make the periods comparable by looking only at ads running in both, matching the number of weekdays, and watching the guardrails. In reports, I wrote “CTR and CVR rose by 15% and 12% after the releases”, rather than “our changes caused a 15% increase”.

07The other half: what could AI + GIS become?

Good timing

I joined around the time of SuperMap's 2025 Spatial Intelligence Software Technology Conference in Beijing, where SuperMap GIS 2025 launched on 24 June. Our platform was built on SuperMap, so my mentor asked me to use the release as a starting point for a more systematic question: what could our product do once AI became part of GIS?

What I studied

I grouped the AI capabilities in SuperMap GIS 2025 into three layers:

  • The foundation: SuperMap AIF, a geospatial AI foundation covering remote-sensing interpretation, spatial analysis, 3D construction, and image and video analysis.
  • AI inside the tools: iDesktopX's new AI assistant (Beta) supported natural-language questions and retrieval, GPA analysis modelling and thematic maps. iServer offered GeoAI machine-learning services, including object detection, land-cover classification, object extraction and geographically weighted regression.
  • Agents: the new SuperMap AgentX Server (Beta) offered knowledge Q&A, workflow-based and autonomous-planning agents. It could connect to open-source models such as Qwen, DeepSeek and ChatGLM, as well as cloud services such as Tongyi Qianwen and ERNIE, and call GIS tools in iServer and iPortal through MCP.

For comparison, I looked at Esri ArcGIS: roughly 100 ready-to-use pretrained models in Living Atlas for tasks such as building-footprint extraction, object detection and land-cover change, alongside AI assistants embedded in ArcGIS products to generate queries, code and operations from natural language. I also studied China's MapGIS and visualisation products such as Alibaba Cloud DataV. For those, the focus was less on AI and more on how map visualisations and decision-support screens were presented, and who used them.

My first impression was that everyone was trying to replace menu-clicking with conversation. The difference was whether AI lived in a chat box or became part of a workflow.

The use cases I proposed

I started with users' daily work and its slow, repetitive parts, then matched those needs to SuperMap's capabilities. That led to four scenarios:

  1. Find and create maps through natural language. An operations or management user might ask for last month's device alerts in a district, grouped by street. Today, that could mean asking a technical colleague to configure layers and write a query. A workflow-based agent could connect understanding the question, running a spatial query and producing a thematic map, so users without GIS skills could get the map themselves.
  2. Map IoT alerts and generate a summary. The company's device alerts already carried location data. Clustering them on a map, adding a heatmap and generating a daily summary of unusual activity and possible relationships could make the information easier to read than a table.
  3. Identify urban incidents. Use image and video analysis to flag issues such as blocked roads, flooding or dumped rubbish, creating incident pins for human confirmation before dispatch.
  4. Support spatial decisions. For site selection and equipment placement, combine spatial methods such as geographically weighted regression with population and POI data to suggest locations and explain them, leaving the final choice to people.

My product recommendations

  • Start with workflows before autonomous planning. Smart-city users were mostly government and enterprise clients. They needed explainable results and repeatable processes. Agents with fixed workflows would be easier to control and deploy first.
  • Keep a human confirmation step for decisions and dispatch. AI could flag a problem or suggest an option, but people should decide who to send and where to act.
  • Make every conclusion traceable to data. Maps can make information look especially convincing. That made source labels, update times and a clear distinction between data and model inference even more important.
  • Start with the slowest, most repetitive task. The most impressive demo might not be the most useful product. Saving someone half an hour every day mattered more.

This part of the work did not ship. The output was a research report with a competitor and capability comparison, user stories and functional journeys for the four scenarios, their SuperMap dependencies, and these recommendations. I shared it with the team in August. My mentor felt the alert-mapping and summary scenario was the closest fit for the company's existing IoT data and added it to future planning discussions. It had not become an approved project when I left.

08What I took from it

1. Define every stage of the funnel

At first, I thought a metrics framework meant building a few charts. In practice, definitions took the most work. What counted as a valid impression? Did two clicks by one person count twice? Was closing a half-loaded page a bounce? Without those answers, every later number was up for argument.

I now think a number is only as trustworthy as the definition behind it.

2. Ads borrow the user's attention

A map is a tool. People open it to find their way or find a place, not to look at ads. We were careful when making the pins more visible: help interested people spot them, without making everyone else fight to avoid them.

That's why we needed guardrails. You can buy a higher click rate by being louder, but sooner or later you pay for it with trust.

3. Removing things is harder than adding them

Simplifying the landing page wasn't technically difficult. Persuading people was. Every module was there because someone once thought it mattered. I learned to bring the conversation back to the one thing a user needed to do on that screen, rather than argue about whether a module was important. Once we agreed on that task, deciding what to remove became easier.

4. An intern's product work is about moving decisions forward

I couldn't decide whether a proposal shipped. I could make the problem clear, lay out the evidence, write requirements that engineering could act on, and show the numbers after release. In other words, make it easier for the people deciding to make good decisions.

That felt a little disappointing at first. Later, it became reassuring: I could see a small part of my work in those 15% and 12% figures.

09A final thought

When I left at the end of August, the journey had become a little easier: pins were clearer, clicks got a response, pages loaded faster and buttons were closer to hand. None of the changes was dramatic. Each came from working through the evidence, one detail at a time.

I was less certain about the AI + GIS report. I didn't know whether those scenarios would ever leave the page. In September 2025, Tencent Maps announced its Gaia spatiotemporal intelligence platform, bringing together large language models, spatiotemporal data and industry agents. Reading the news, I thought we were probably looking in a useful direction. The remaining question was who would turn it into something people truly relied on.

The most important lesson from that summer might be this:

Behind every pin on a map is someone hoping to be found, and someone looking. A product's job is to bring them a few steps closer.
